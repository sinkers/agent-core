# Choosing a delivery model, ECI and GMP, and amending a standard form

Sources: Turtons (Sydney construction lawyers), "The different types of construction contract explained" (G. Henry,
14 Dec 2018) and its linked articles on ECI, AS 4916, provisional sums, common amendments to AS 4000 and AS 2124, and
rise and fall clauses (turtons.com/blog). Law-firm commentary from 2018, NSW-based: it predates
AS 4000:2025, which now has its own GST, WHS principal-contractor and PPSA clauses. Check the actual form and the
project's special conditions; contractual decisions go to a person and the organisation's lawyer.

## Three questions that pick the model

1. **Must work start, or orders be placed, before the design is complete?** Yes → ECI (or construction management)
   is the practical route; scope and price are less certain at signing. Long-lead plant (transformers, PTUs,
   switchgear) is a common reason.
2. **Is the builder's input wanted on the design before the price is fixed?** Buildability, value engineering and
   design management help on complex or budget-tight work.
3. **Should the principal keep control of the consultants?** Principal-engaged consultants keep control of the design and
   avoid contractor-driven compromises. Contractor-engaged consultants (directly or by novation) give a single point
   of responsibility for defects, with no finger-pointing between builder and designer.

Design complete, time not critical → a lump sum tender gives competition and price certainty. Design mostly complete
with a few gaps → lump sum with provisional sums for the gaps.

| Model | Forms | Price basis | Who carries trade risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lump sum construct only | AS 4000 (2025 or 1997), AS 2124 | Fixed for agreed scope | Contractor (liable for subcontractors as for itself) |
| Lump sum design and construct | AS 4902, AS 4300 | Fixed; fitness for stated purpose | Contractor, including design |
| ECI, construct only | Bespoke ECI agreement, then amended AS 4000 / AS 2124 | Fee in stage 1, then lump sum or cost-plus | Contractor once converted |
| ECI, design and construct | Bespoke ECI agreement, then amended AS 4902 / AS 4300 | As above | Contractor once converted, including design |
| Managing contractor | No standard form: amended AS 4000 / AS 2124 (or AS 4902 / AS 4300 with design) plus an ECI agreement | Fee + actual third-party cost, or converted to a lump sum | **Contractor** (head contractor, liable for its trades) |
| Construction management | **AS 4916-2002**, trade contracts **AS 4917-2003** | Fee (Annexure Part C: lump sum, % or rates) | **The principal** (trades contract with it) |

There is no Australian Standard ECI agreement: it's always bespoke.

## ECI

- The contractor is engaged, and may start, before the design is finished. It's paid a fee for preliminaries, overhead
  and profit; the construction cost isn't known at signing. It typically runs the trade tenders **open book**.
- Benefits: early packages and long-lead orders, builder input on design, transparent subcontract pricing, a shorter
  overall programme.
- Principal's risks: less cost certainty at engagement; reliance on the contractor's honesty, since it advises on
  budget and runs the trade tenders with no competing head-contract tenderers.
- Contractor's tension: it acts first like a consultant to the principal, then as a party with its own commercial interest.
- **Conversion to a lump sum**, by either (a) one full lump sum proposal when the design is developed, which the principal may
  accept, reject or negotiate; or (b) progressive pricing of trade packages, with risk allowances, totalled into the
  lump sum. The agreement must say exactly how and when conversion happens, and what happens if it doesn't (who owns
  the design and IP, what stage 1 is paid, whether the principal can go to market).
- Name the agreement for what it does: the payment and conversion terms matter, not the title.

## Guaranteed maximum price (GMP)

Usually a managing-contractor arrangement where the contractor uses best efforts to stay under a nominated price. The
"guarantee" is rarely absolute: early information can't support it without big risk premiums, and the price still
moves for principal-directed variations and events outside the contractor's control (latent conditions). Define
exactly which events adjust the GMP, how savings are shared, and what costs are reimbursable.

## Construction management (AS 4916)

- The construction manager (CM) acts as **the principal's agent**, with fiduciary duties (act transparently, in the principal's best
  interests). Trade contracts are between the trade contractor and the principal, even if the CM signs as agent; the principal often
  pays trades directly on the CM's recommendation.
- Compared with a lump sum head contractor, the CM carries little risk: no obligation to reach practical completion
  by a date, **no liquidated damages**, EOTs and delay costs for trade-caused delay, not liable for trade defaults or
  defects it didn't cause, **no security**, and no equivalent of AS 4000 cl 14 (care of the works and injury risk).
  The principal carries trade-contractor default and insolvency risk, so it needs a strong owner team.
- The CM's obligations are "the Services" in **Annexure Part B**. The default pick list has an "agent" / "not as
  agent" column for each task, and it causes problems:
  - ticking "not as agent" can strip the fiduciary duty, transparency and security for that task, block LDs, limit
    the CM's EOT rights and leave latent-condition risk unclear;
  - ticking everything "not as agent" does **not** reliably move construction risk to the CM; it mostly creates
    disputes;
  - fix it with a bespoke task list, or delete the two headings and tick only the tasks wanted. If the CM is meant to
    carry delivery risk, it isn't traditional CM: amend by special conditions or use an ECI agreement with a lump sum
    (AS 4000 or AS 4902).
- AS 4916 and AS 4917 need a Standards Australia licence. AS 4915 is the matching project-management (consultancy)
  form.

## Provisional sums and prime cost items

- A provisional sum is an allowance for work that can't be priced at signing; it's adjusted to actual cost. A prime
  cost item traditionally covers supply only; in AS 4000 and AS 2124 the "provisional sum" definition includes prime
  cost items, so they're treated alike (AS 4000 cl 3, AS 2124 cl 11).
- **Margin varies by contract**: some apply the margin percentage to the whole actual cost, others only to the excess
  over the allowance (assuming the fixed price already covers profit and attendance). Commentators describe the
  unamended forms differently: check the executed contract's clause and Annexure before stating which applies.
- Disputes come from vague descriptions (is this work inside the provisional sum or the fixed price?). Describe
  provisional-sum work precisely, and say in the tender whether overhead and profit on provisional sums sit in the
  allowance or the fixed price.

## Rise and fall clauses

No standard wording. Decide each of these:
- **Scope**: labour, materials or both; usually limited to named packages or materials (steel, timber, copper,
  transformers) so most of the lump sum stays fixed. If actual cost is the intent, use a cost-reimbursable model
  instead.
- **Mechanism**: tendered vs actual cost (labour often by award-rate changes), or an index formula. Name the index,
  usually an ABS Producer Price Index, with a fallback (ABS CPI) if it stops being published.
- **Base date**: tender date or contract date (a long gap between them matters).
- **Frequency**: monthly, quarterly, 6- or 12-monthly, or once at the end; and bar claims outside those times.
- **Thresholds**: no adjustment for the first 6 or 12 months, or until a cost moves by x %; say whether the
  adjustment is on the whole cost or only above the threshold. Keep thresholds modest or the clause does nothing.
- Make it work both ways (falling as well as rising prices).
- Rise and fall usually adjusts cost, not margin; provisional sums often adjust margin too.

## Amending a standard form: the usual list

Most principals amend even a plain AS 4000. Each item shifts risk to the contractor, and the risk gets priced:
over-amending adds risk premiums or puts tenderers off, so target risks the contractor can actually manage, and
consider the market.

| Amendment | AS 4000-1997 clause | AS 2124 clause | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| Make notice time bars hard bars (unamended, late notice only gives damages) | 41.1, 41.2 | — | Principal |
| Narrow EOT and cost relief: fewer qualifying/compensable causes; narrow or exclude latent conditions; limit relief for design discrepancies; add buildability risk | definitions, 25, 8 | 35.5, 12, 8.1 | Principal |
| Cap delay costs; set variation rates and margins; no margin on provisional-sum adjustments; no off-site overhead or profit on latent-condition work | 34.9, 36, 3, 25 | 36, 40, 11, 12 | Principal |
| Delete the deemed EOT (claim deemed granted if the superintendent doesn't answer in 28 days) | 34.5 | — | Principal |
| Shorten notice periods (e.g. the 28-day EOT claim) | 34.3 | 35.5 | Principal; keep them workable or notice disputes follow |
| Variations only by written direction, with a set time to notify a claim | — (1997 already requires writing, 36.1) | 40 | Principal |
| Unilateral EOT power is for the principal's benefit only (protects LDs; watch the prevention principle) | 34.5 with 20 | 35.5 with 23 | Principal |
| GST clause (forms predate GST; AS 4000:2025 has one) | new | new | Both |
| Proportionate liability excluded; contractor appointed WHS principal contractor; PPSA clause over unfixed plant and materials (AS 4000:2025 covers WHS and PPSA) | new | new | Principal |
| Security of payment: can only supplement the Act, never contract out | new | new | — |
| Subcontractor and supplier undertakings at the start, and warranty deeds at practical completion, in an annexed form (closes the privity gap if the head contractor fails) | new | new | Principal |
| Rise and fall (see above) | new | new | Contractor's pricing risk falls |

Contractors commonly ask for: a cap on liability, an exclusion of consequential loss, and a rise and fall clause.
